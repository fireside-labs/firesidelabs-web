Why You Can't Shrink Gemma 4 or Qwen 3 the Easy Way

A Negative Result on Weight-Space Expert Redundancy in 128-Expert Top-8 MoE Models

Cosine-similarity-based expert merging is a published technique for shrinking Mixture-of-Experts models with minimal capability loss. The theory: trained MoE models often have redundant experts that can be clustered by weight similarity and merged. We tested this on two modern fine-grained MoE models—Google's gemma-4-26B-A4B (128 experts top-8) and Alibaba's Qwen3-30B-A3B (128 experts top-8)—and found that experts in both models are essentially orthogonal.

Maximum pairwise cosine similarity is 0.053 (Gemma) and 0.010 (Qwen3); mean similarity is 0.000–0.004. No clusters form at any similarity threshold down to 0.50.

Cosine-merge cannot reduce these models without modification. Orthogonality isn't a bug in modern MoE training—it's a feature, designed in by 128 small experts competing under strict top-K routing.

This is a useful negative result for practitioners considering MoE size reduction: weight-level approaches don't work on this architecture family. Functional approaches—utilization-based pruning, output-similarity merging, distillation—remain viable.

The Hypothesis Behind Cosine-Merge

Trained MoE models have a router that learns to send tokens to specialized experts. In theory, the router could learn redundant assignments: multiple experts learning highly correlated representations because the gradient signal didn't separate them strongly enough during training. Cosine-merge surgery exploits this hypothesized redundancy through a five-step process.

01

Compute pairwise cosine similarity between every pair of expert weight vectors in a given MoE block.

02

Cluster experts at some similarity threshold (e.g., similarity > 0.85).

03

Merge each cluster into a single expert via weighted average of weights, optionally weighted by router probability over a calibration set.

04

Update the router to point to the new merged expert IDs instead of the originals.

05

Ship the smaller model with most capability preserved.

Published work has reported this technique working on Mixtral-8x7B (8 experts top-2) and earlier DeepSeek MoE variants, achieving 25–40% size reduction with <2pt benchmark drop. The natural question: does it work on larger, more recent fine-grained MoE designs?

Setup

We tested two modern 128-expert top-8 MoE models, both quantized to Q4_K_M:

Model Stored Per-token Architecture
gemma-4-26B-A4B-it 26B ~7B 30 blocks, 128 experts/block, top-8, 704-dim, shared FFN
Qwen3-30B-A3B 30B ~3B 48 blocks, 128 experts/block, top-8, 768-dim, no shared FFN

For each block, we loaded all 128 routed experts, dequantized their weights to FP32, and built a per-expert "fingerprint" by concatenating the expert's gate, up, and down projection weights into a single vector (~5.9M dimensions for Gemma, ~4.7M for Qwen3). We then computed pairwise cosine similarity matrices and analyzed the distribution.

We sampled 5 blocks per model (evenly spaced across model depth) to check whether expert redundancy varies by layer.

What These Numbers Mean

Cosine similarity ranges from −1 to +1. Values near 0 mean two vectors are essentially perpendicular—they share no directional relationship. A threshold like 0.85 (used in Mixtral-era cosine-merge work) finds expert pairs that point in nearly the same direction. At 0.053 maximum across 128×127/2 = 8,128 pairs per block, every expert in Gemma-4-26B points in a meaningfully different direction from every other expert.

Why This Happens

128-expert top-8 architectures route each token through 8 experts simultaneously, with the router learning a specialization weight per expert. The gradient pressure during training pushes each expert toward a distinct subspace of the residual stream. Three mechanisms compound:

Mechanism 01
Many small experts, not a few large ones

With 128 experts each at 704-dim FFN, each expert has limited capacity—forcing it to specialize narrowly. There is no "leftover capacity" to learn redundant patterns. The earlier MoE architectures where cosine-merge worked (Mixtral 8x7B, DeepSeek-V1) had 8–32 large experts at 1.5–4B parameters each, where each expert covered broader patterns. Broader patterns admit more redundancy.

Mechanism 02
Top-K routing competition

The router has to pick the best 8 experts per token. If two experts learn correlated representations, they compete for the same routing decisions, and the gradient signal pushes them apart. Orthogonality is what the training objective selects for.

Mechanism 03
Training scale washes out initial correlations

Both Google and Alibaba train these MoEs on enormous corpora with sufficient gradient steps that any initial expert correlations get washed out. The orthogonality observed at equilibrium is downstream of trillions of training tokens of expert-vs-expert competition.

What This Means for Practitioners

Don't try cosine-merge on Gemma 4 / Qwen 3

The redundancy isn't there at the weight level. Any cosine-merge pipeline applied to these architectures will either find no clusters at usable thresholds, or merge experts that aren't actually redundant and silently degrade capability.

Older MoE families may still admit the technique

If you're working with Mixtral-8x7B variants, DeepSeek-V1 variants, or other 8–32 expert top-2/4 designs, cosine-merge is still a viable size-reduction tool. The technique isn't wrong; it's just architecturally narrow.

Functional similarity is a different question

Two experts can have orthogonal weights and still produce correlated outputs on real input distributions—because an expert's effective behavior is determined by which inputs route to it, not just by its weight orientation. Merging based on output similarity over a calibration set is an open question that this weight-space analysis didn't test.

Utilization pruning is a separate path

Even if all experts are weight-distinct, some may rarely fire on representative workloads. Dropping low-utilization experts can still shrink the model. We did not test this here, but it remains a viable size-reduction approach orthogonal (so to speak) to the cosine-merge question.

Implications for Benchmark Interpretation

Architecture comparisons that pit "30B MoE" against "8B dense" without specifying the MoE design—8 experts top-2 versus 128 experts top-8—are missing important variance. Modern fine-grained MoE has different size-reduction properties than older designs. The "MoE family" is not a single architectural choice; it's a spectrum with markedly different surgery affordances.

Practitioners reading a "MoE surgery succeeded" paper should ask which MoE generation it applied to before assuming it transfers. The answer is more often "a different one" than the summary makes obvious.

Reproduction

Analysis script: moe_cosine_analyze.py. Single command:

python moe_cosine_analyze.py \ --gguf <path/to/model.gguf> \ --blocks 0,7,14,21,28

Each block analysis takes ~2–3 seconds (after a one-time tensor load of ~10s) on a workstation with 32GB+ RAM. Both models tested fit in memory layer-by-layer.

The script computes pairwise cosine similarity and reports cluster counts at thresholds 0.85 / 0.80 / … / 0.50. For these models, the answer at every threshold is identical: 128 clusters, all size 1—no merging possible.

Limitations

Two models tested

We checked gemma-4-26B-A4B and Qwen3-30B-A3B. Other 128-expert designs (DeepSeek-V2, hypothetical future Mixtral-8x22B extensions) would strengthen the claim that this is a general property of fine-grained MoE.

Q4 dequantization adds noise

We worked from Q4_K_M GGUF files, dequantized to FP32. Quantization may have shifted some weight similarities, but the magnitudes we observed (max 0.053) are far from any reasonable merging threshold; the signal direction is robust to quantization noise.

5 blocks sampled per model

We checked blocks evenly spaced across model depth and found consistent orthogonality. Full-coverage analysis (every block) would be conclusive. At 4-second-per-block analysis time this is straightforward to extend if needed.

Weight cosine is not the only similarity metric

Functional similarity (do these two experts compute similar functions on real inputs?) was not measured here. That's a follow-up question, and the answer could plausibly differ from the weight-space answer.

Negative result on a specific technique, not on MoE surgery in general

Other approaches—utilization pruning, output-based merging, distillation, expert addition— remain viable. This paper says cosine-merge specifically doesn't apply to fine-grained MoE; it doesn't say no surgery works.

What's Next

Output-similarity analysis (functional, not weight-based)

Hook the router during inference, log expert output activations on a calibration set, compute similarity of expert behaviors rather than weights. This could find merge candidates that weight-space analysis misses. ~1 day of work.

Utilization pruning as the alternative size-reduction path

Log per-expert routing probability over a calibration set, drop bottom-N% utilization experts. ~half-day implementation, real-day evaluation.

Expand the survey

Test cosine-merge applicability on DeepSeek-V2, Mixtral variants, and future fine-grained models as they become available. The 128-expert orthogonality claim deserves replication across vendors before being treated as a general law.

Modern fine-grained MoE is architecturally designed to make weight-space expert merging impossible. This paper's value is preventing wasted engineering on the wrong size-reduction approach. The negative result is the result.
