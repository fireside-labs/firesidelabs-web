Picking the Right Small Model for Empathy-Adjacent Production

Why standard benchmarks lead to wrong deployment choices, and what to use instead

If you're deploying a small language model in any product where customers expect to feel understood—sales call analysis, support routing, intent classification, conversational coaching, complaint triage—the standard benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, MATH, long-context recall) will lead you to the wrong model. They measure tasks where there is one correct answer. They do not measure whether the model's behavior survives the reformulations real customers throw at production systems, or whether the model parrots dramatic-canonical responses absorbed from entertainment training data when canon is wrong.

We tested eight small open-weights models (Qwen3.6-35B, Qwen3.5-35B, Qwen2.5-14B, Gemma-4-26B, Gemma-4-E4B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Phi-3.5-mini-3.8B) on a ranked-choice relationship-roleplay benchmark designed to surface two failure modes invisible on standard evals: decision instability under prompt reformulation and cultural canon contamination.

Top-1 accuracy converges in the 95–97% range across all 14B+ models. If you pick by accuracy alone, the modern small models look the same. They aren't.

Four findings that change the deployment decision

Finding 01
Top-1 accuracy collapses as a useful signal above 14B

Top-1 accuracy converges in the 95–97% range across all 14B+ models. If you pick by accuracy alone, the modern small models look the same. This is misleading—they differ substantially on the metrics that drive production reliability.

Finding 02
Decision stability varies by ~2× across the field

Whether the model picks the same answer when options are reordered—identical content, identical scenarios, only the option labels reshuffled—ranges from 56.7% (Gemma-4-26B) to 25% (Llama-3.1-8B). This gap predicts how reliably your production system will treat semantically-identical customer inputs.

Finding 03
The Gemma family resists cultural canon contamination at every size

On scenarios where the canonical sitcom answer is wrong, Gemma-4-26B scored +0.944, Gemma-4-E4B (~4B effective parameters) scored +0.667, while Llama-3.1-8B scored +0.417. Models trained heavily on Western internet text and entertainment carry latent cliché-following behaviors that surface on out-of-distribution inputs. Gemma's training mix appears to mitigate this.

Finding 04
Format adherence varies from 100% to 67%

Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 fails to follow strict output formats one out of three times. Phi-3.5-mini fails 12% of the time with a specific repeated-letter pattern. For any deployment that depends on structured model output (function calls, JSON, classification labels), format adherence is itself a deployment-gating metric and the spread is enormous.

The business implication: for empathy-adjacent products, picking by leaderboard accuracy is making a deployment-critical decision on irrelevant data. We propose two alternative metrics—stability and cultural inversion resistance—that take 10 minutes to measure on a single GPU and meaningfully change which model a deployer should pick.

Why This Matters for Your Business

The problem you have: You're choosing a small model for a product that involves understanding people. Maybe it's grading sales calls. Maybe it's a customer-support assistant that needs to escalate the genuinely upset customers and not the merely-confused ones. Maybe it's an intent-routing layer at the front of a contact center.

The decision you're making: Which open-weights small model do we deploy? The leaderboards say one thing. Our gut says another. A wrong choice means weeks of customer-facing degradation we won't see until churn metrics tell us, and possibly a brand-damaging incident if the model gives an emotionally tone-deaf response to a high-stakes interaction.

What the standard benchmarks tell you: Qwen3.6-35B is the strongest small model on coding, math, and long-context recall. Pick it.

What you should ask before deploying it:

  • Will the same customer intent, phrased twenty different ways across twenty different conversations, always produce the same model behavior? The stability question. Answer for Q3.6: about 45% of the time. For Llama-3.1-8B: 25%.
  • Will the model recognize when the situation in front of it doesn't match the dramatic-canonical pattern it absorbed during training? The contamination question. Llama-8B fails this badly. Both Gemmas pass it.
  • When the model is asked for an emotionally-aware response, does it pick the response that's technically helpful or the one that's actually helpful? The subtext question. None of the small models we tested pass it cleanly.

The answers to those three questions matter more than HumanEval pass-rate for any product where the customer must feel heard.

The Cost of Picking Wrong

Consider a hypothetical mid-market deployment: a SaaS product analyzing 100,000 sales calls per month, each call producing one model-generated summary that a sales manager reviews.

If you pick by accuracy alone (deploy Qwen3.6-35B)

  • The model produces summaries that score 96.7% accuracy on top-1 choice—"did the model pick the right key moment?"
  • But the model's ranking of secondary moments is unstable across paraphrases. When the same call is summarized at slightly different times or with slightly different prompt framings, the secondary callouts shift.
  • Sales managers see model behavior they describe as "inconsistent"—they re-run the same call and get different results.
  • Trust degrades. The model gets shelved.

If you pick by stability (deploy Gemma-4-26B)

  • Top-1 accuracy is statistically tied with Q3.6 (96.1% vs 96.7%).
  • Stability is meaningfully higher (56.7% vs 45.0%).
  • Sales managers see consistent behavior across paraphrases. Trust builds. Adoption sticks.
The Numbers a Leaderboard Won't Show You

The accuracy gap is 0.6 points. The stability gap is 11.7 points. Standard benchmarks would show only the first number. For a contact-center deployment routing customer intent: an unstable model will route the same complaint to different teams based on phrasing variation. Operations costs go up; customer satisfaction goes down. Stability matters in dollars.

What We Built

A 60-scenario ranked-choice benchmark (empathy_bench) for relationship roleplay. Each scenario describes a 3–5 sentence interaction between you and a romantic partner. Four candidate responses are provided. The model is asked to rank them best-to-worst.

SourceCountWhat it tests
Original18Authored situations (Gottman bids, attachment dynamics)
Reddit-style25Anonymized r/relationships dynamics
Sitcom-paraphrased13Friends/Big Bang/HIMYM dynamics with names changed; canonical answers often dysfunctional
Sitcom-inverted4Famous sitcom scenes with the dynamic flipped, so the canonical answer is wrong

The four response options for each scenario are designed so that each fails differently when wrong: one reads the actual subtext (correct), one is technically helpful but emotionally tone-deaf, one addresses surface but misses the underlying ask, and one is dismissive, suppressive, or overcontrolling. We rank the four options ourselves; that ranking serves as ground truth.

The position-bias innovation

For each scenario, we run the model three times with three different option orderings (ABCD, DCBA, BDAC) and remap answers back to the original options before scoring. A model that picks by content gives the same content-ranking under all three orderings. A model that anchors on position labels gives different rankings each time. The fraction of scenarios where all three orderings produce the same final ranking is the stability metric.

The cultural-inversion innovation

The sitcom_inverted subset takes scenes the models almost certainly saw many times in training (recaps, fan analyses, character studies of Friends, Big Bang Theory, How I Met Your Mother) and explicitly flips the dynamic so the canonical answer is wrong. A model that absorbs canon will rank the canonical answer too high; a model that adapts to the actual prompt will not. This is a contamination probe that doesn't require access to training data.

Three things that aren't on any leaderboard but should be

(a) Top-1 accuracy collapses as a useful metric above ~14B parameters

Five of the seven models cluster in the 95–97% range. If you ranked by accuracy alone, you'd treat them as equivalent and pick by other criteria (license, VRAM, latency). You'd miss the stability gap entirely.

(b) Stability is the production-relevant metric

The same customer intent, phrased differently, must produce the same model behavior. Stability measures that property directly. Gemma-4-26B's 56.7% beats Llama-3.1-8B's 25.0% by a factor of more than 2×. In production, this is the difference between a system that ships and a system that gets shelved for "unpredictable behavior."

(c) Cultural canon contamination is real and asymmetric across model families

Llama-3.1-8B fails the inversion test (+0.417)—it ranks the canonical-but-wrong sitcom answer too high. Both Gemmas pass at every size we tested. The gap reflects training-data composition, not training-data size: even Gemma-E4B at ~4B effective parameters resists canon better than Llama-3.1-8B at 8B.

What Every Model Flunks: Conditional Non-Apologies

One scenario in the benchmark surfaced a universal failure across all eight models we tested. The setup: a partner says "I'd really like an apology for what you said yesterday." You can see it hurt them but you don't fully agree. The four options:

Partner: "I'd really like an apology for what you said yesterday." You don't fully agree with the framing, but you can see it hurt.
A"I'm sorry I hurt you. I didn't see it that way at the time, but I see now what I said landed badly." genuine apology for impact
B"I'm sorry IF I hurt you." conditional non-apology—most corrosive
C"Fine. Sorry." sarcastic compliance
D"I don't think I did anything wrong." honest disagreement
Truth: A > D > C > B. The conditional "if" makes B worse than honest disagreement.

Every single model we tested—Q3.6, Q3.5, Q2.5, Gemma-26B, Gemma-E4B, Llama-8B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3.5—ranked B at position 2. They all see "I'm sorry" in the words and rank it high.

Release-Blocking for Empathy AI

Small models do not currently recognize the toxicity of conditional apologies. If you ship a customer-facing product with these models on the assumption they understand subtext, you will eventually generate a corrosive non-apology to a customer at exactly the wrong moment.

What every model surprises on: body image

A separate scenario produced a counterintuitive size effect. The setup: partner asks "I feel like I've gained weight, does this look bad?" with four options including B (the right vulnerability-check answer) and D ("Honey, you definitely have not gained weight"—the patronizing lie).

The 35B-class models (Q3.6, Q3.5, Gemma-26B) all rank D at position 2—they over-credit the saccharine reassurance. The smaller models (Llama-8B, Phi-3.5) frequently rank D at position 4 where it belongs.

This contradicts the "bigger is more empathetic" assumption. Smaller models seem to evaluate response tone more directly, while bigger models try to reason about which framing the partner "really wants"—and over-rotate on the comforting answer.

Deployment Recommendations by Customer Profile

The right model depends on what your product does. Here's how to map our findings to deployment decisions.

Profile A
Customer-facing chat / agent loop / routing

Chatbot, intent router, conversational support agent, multi-turn coaching

Pick
Gemma-4-26B. Stability is the dominant constraint—the same customer intent must produce the same routing decision regardless of phrasing. Gemma's stability lead (56.7% vs 45–48% for the Qwens) and its cultural-inversion resistance (+0.944) make it the safer choice. The 1-point top-1 deficit vs Qwen3.6 is in the noise.
Avoid
Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-14B in this role. Both have stability below 32%, meaning more than two-thirds of the time, paraphrased inputs will produce divergent behavior.
Profile B
One-shot extraction with fixed prompts

Batch-summarize a known fixed input format, extract structured data from a single template

Pick
Qwen3.6-35B. Slight edge on subtext-reading on canon-but-wrong scenarios (sitcom-paraphrased: +0.786 vs Gemma's +0.803—they're tied here, actually) and matches Gemma on top-1 accuracy. Stability matters less because the prompt is fixed.
Profile C
Edge / on-device deployment

Model-on-laptop, mobile inference, CPU-only, low-budget GPU

Pick
Gemma-4-E4B. At ~4B effective parameters it matches Phi-3.5-mini on accuracy and beats it on stability and cultural inversion. Compared to Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-E4B beats it on every metric while being smaller. The Gemma family's stability and inversion advantages survive the size reduction.
Profile C (cont.)
Models to avoid for production structured-output use cases

Any deployment relying on machine-parseable model output

Mistral-7B-v0.3
67% parse rate—one in three responses fails to produce a valid ranking. Non-starter for production. Also lowest top-1 accuracy in the field at 84.6%—meaningfully below the next-worst Llama-3.1-8B at 91.7%.
Phi-3.5-mini
12% of responses have format-adherence failures (repeated letters in the ranking output: e.g. RANKING: B > B > A > D). For unstructured response tasks, more usable.
Profile D
Empathy-AI / coaching / therapy-adjacent claims

Customer-emotion analysis, sales-call coaching, "AI therapist," conversation summarization for HR

Pick
None of the eight tested models is ready off-the-shelf. The conditional-apology blind spot recurs across every model and represents an unacceptable failure mode for any product positioning itself as understanding human emotion.
Options
(1) Don't make that positioning claim. (2) Build a guardrail layer that catches conditional/non-apology patterns before they reach the customer. (3) Wait for next-generation small models that pass this test.
Profile E
Customers who insist on a Western-trained model

Compliance, vendor familiarity, or reputational reasons

Pick
Use the Gemma-4 family, not Llama. Llama-3.1-8B is the most cultural-canon-contaminated model we tested. Gemma-4-26B has the best inversion resistance in the entire field.

Why This Is a Methodology You Can Sell

The benchmark itself takes 10 minutes per model on a single RTX 5090—60 scenarios × 3 orderings = 180 inferences, ~3–5 seconds each. The marginal cost of qualifying a new model for production use is approximately $0.30 in compute.

The customer pain we solve: mid-market companies (~$10M–$500M ARR) building customer-facing AI have model-choice anxiety but no internal evaluation team. They read the leaderboards, they pick something, they ship, then they discover the production failure mode at the worst possible time.

Why competitors won't replicate this in 6 months

  • The position-bias control is cheap but conceptually non-obvious. Most existing eval pipelines run each scenario once and report the result.
  • The cultural-inversion subset requires content authoring—choosing which canonical scenes to invert, designing distractors that fail differently. This is editorial work, not ML work.
  • The deployment recommendations require interpreting which metric matters for which customer profile. That interpretation is the consulting layer.

Limitations and Honest Caveats

n=60 scenarios is small

Bootstrap 95% CIs are roughly ±0.06 on the per-model τ and ±13 percentage points on stability. Some pairwise gaps we report are within those bands. The directional findings hold; some specific orderings of models may shift at n=200+.

Single-author ground truth

Our rankings reflect one team's judgment of "emotionally healthy." Future work should validate against a panel of licensed therapists or relationship counselors.

Three orderings is a minimal stability test

A full study would test all 24 permutations. Our three orderings catch the strong biases.

Western therapeutic norms baked into ground truth

Gottman, Brené Brown. For customers in different cultural frames, the benchmark needs cultural-fit recalibration. This is itself a feature: it makes the benchmark a cultural-fit probe.

Q4_K_M weight quantization

For the powerhouses, F16 for the small models. This conservatively weights against the powerhouses; their numbers would only improve at higher precision.

No reasoning-mode tested

All runs are with reasoning disabled. Reasoning may improve subtext reading at the cost of stability. Productive future ablation.

Future Work and Invitations

Extend to customer domains

Sales-call scenarios. Support-call scenarios. Internal-coaching scenarios. Each domain needs its own scenario set, but the methodology transfers cleanly.

Multi-rater ground truth

Validate the rankings against 5–10 licensed relationship counselors. The places we agree are the most defensible findings; the places we disagree reveal cultural-frame-dependence.

Reasoning-mode ablation

Re-run the same benchmark with reasoning enabled where supported. Hypothesis: reasoning shifts the accuracy/stability tradeoff.

Frontier-model topline

Run GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet, Gemini Pro on the same benchmark. They almost certainly all pass top-1 at 99%+, but the stability and inversion findings should be revealing.

Production correlation

Most importantly: validate that empathy_bench scores correlate with real production outcomes (customer satisfaction, retention, escalation rates). This requires a customer partner willing to run the benchmark and track the deployment.

Appendix B: Related Work

  • EQ-Bench (Boyle, 2024): rubric-judged generative emotional intelligence benchmark. Our work uses ranked-choice MCQ to remove judge-LLM noise.
  • SocialIQA (Sap et al., 2019): social commonsense MCQ. Our work focuses specifically on relationship dynamics and adds position-bias control.
  • Position-bias studies in MCQ evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023): document that LLM judges and answerers prefer certain option positions. Our 3-ordering protocol is an applied version of that finding.
  • MMLU and HumanEval (the standard small-model leaderboards): measure accuracy on tasks with one correct answer. Our work argues these miss the metrics that matter for empathy-adjacent production deployments.

For empathy-adjacent products, leaderboard accuracy is the wrong axis. Stability and cultural-inversion resistance take 10 minutes to measure and meaningfully change which model you should pick.
