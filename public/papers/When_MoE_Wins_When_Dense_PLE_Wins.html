16 Points Separate the Wrong Architecture From the Right One

A Compute-Matched Empirical Study of Small Language Model Architectures Across Single-Question and Multi-Step Benchmarks

Abstract

We measured five small language models across four benchmarks under a fixed harness, with three replicate runs per model on a noisy multi-step bash workflow benchmark. Three findings drop out cleanly. First, at matched per-token compute (~3–4B activated), MoE and dense+PLE architectures tie within ±3pt across single-question benchmarks (TruthfulQA, HellaSwag, GSM8K). Second, on multi-step bash workflows the same matched-compute pair shows a 16.3pt first-try gap favoring dense+PLE (4σ confidence with n=3 each); the with-retry gap closes to 6pt and is within harness noise. Third, at higher per-token compute (7B activated), the larger MoE substantially outperforms the smaller dense+PLE on multi-step (+10.7pt first-try, +12pt retry, 3.5σ).

The unified picture: architecture choice depends on compute tier, not on architecture family in the abstract. At matched compute the small MoE has a routing-disadvantage on first-attempt multi-step; raise the compute level and the larger MoE overcomes it.

We additionally report (i) a 5σ result that a 4B-effective dense+PLE model dominates a 14B vanilla dense model by 16–17pt on multi-step, suggesting the Per-Layer Embeddings architecture punches well above its compute weight, and (ii) a methodological finding that the WildClaw multi-step benchmark exhibits 25–35% per-task pass/fail flip rate at temp=0 only when thinking-mode bleed is permitted—explicit chat_template_kwargs.enable_thinking=False muzzling produces fully deterministic first-try behavior (std=0 across n=3 same-config runs). Architecture comparisons should use explicit muzzle for apples-to-apples; production-realistic deployment estimates should use thinking-on with n≥3.

The Question

Published architecture comparisons typically pit "30B MoE" against "8B dense" with the implicit claim that the MoE wins because of total parameters. But the MoE only activates a fraction of those per token. The honest unit for comparing capability is per-token compute: how many parameters do real math each forward pass. Under that lens, a 30B MoE that activates 3B/token compares to an 8B dense model rather than to a 30B dense one.

Total parameters is a memory metric. Per-token compute is a capability metric. Get the unit right before you start comparing architectures.

Two practitioner questions follow. First: at matched per-token compute, does architecture still matter? Second: does the answer depend on the benchmark class—single-question vs multi-step?

Four Findings, Unified

Finding 01 HIGH CONFIDENCE
Single-question matched-compute tie

At matched per-token compute, MoE and dense+PLE tie on three single-question benchmarks within ±3pt. Architecture is essentially neutral for short tasks at matched compute.

Finding 02 4σ
Small-MoE first-try gap on multi-step

At matched per-token compute on multi-step bash workflows, the 3B-activated MoE underperforms the 4B dense+PLE on first-try success rate by 16.3pt. The with-retry gap closes to 6pt and is within harness noise. First-try is the metric that matters for agent-loop performance—most production agent systems retry expensively or not at all—so the 16pt first-try gap is the practitioner-relevant claim.

Finding 03 3.5σ
Large-MoE breaks the hypothesis

At higher per-token compute (7B activated), the larger MoE substantially outperforms the smaller dense+PLE on multi-step (+10.7pt first-try, +12pt retry). gemma-4-26b-moe is the strongest WildClaw model in our entire fleet at 78% retry / 65% first-try. The "MoE breaks at long context" claim is wrong as architecture-general—it is specifically a small-MoE / matched-compute phenomenon. Raise the compute and the architecture wins.

Finding 04 5σ
PLE punches above its compute weight

A 4B-effective dense+PLE model (gemma4-E4B) dominates a 14B vanilla dense model (qwen2.5-14b-vanilla) on WildClaw English by 16–17pt across both metrics. Per-Layer Embeddings is doing something architecturally that lets it deploy multi-step capability disproportionate to its activated compute count. We do not yet have a mechanistic explanation for this; we report it as a phenomenon worth further investigation.

Why the Small-MoE First-Try Gap Is the Right Framing

The MoE routing-divergence hypothesis predicts that on long contexts, different tokens may route through different expert mixtures, introducing per-step non-determinism that compounds over multi-step tasks. If the model's first-attempt plan goes off-rails because of routing instability, the entire bash workflow fails on that attempt. Retries can recover sometimes—by retrying, the model gets a fresh routing trajectory and may produce different (potentially better) output.

This predicts exactly the asymmetry we observe: the first-try metric is most sensitive to routing-divergence, and that is where we see the large effect (16pt). With-retry score smooths over individual routing mistakes, so that gap is in the noise.

The larger-MoE result is consistent with this story: at matched compute the small MoE is at a routing disadvantage; at higher compute the larger MoE has enough capacity per token to overcome it.

Crucially, this isn't just "more compute helps" (which would predict a magnitude difference, not a direction reversal). The gemma-4-26b-moe at 7B activated has more capacity per token, and possibly tighter routing under long context. The two effects compound.

Methodological Finding: WildClaw Noise Floor Is Thinking-Mode-Conditional

Three same-config runs (workers=2, temp=0, identical flags) on gemma4-E4B with thinking enabled (default Gemma channel format):

Run 1: 28 first-try, 33 retry Run 2: 33 first-try, 41 retry Run 3: 23 first-try, 31 retry Mean ± std: 28 ± 5 first-try, 35 ± 5 retry Range: 10 tasks (~17pt) for both metrics

Pairwise agreement between any two of three runs: 73–80%. 20–27% of tasks flip pass/fail between identical-config runs at temp=0 in this configuration. Across all three runs, only 38% of tasks always pass, 27% always fail, and 35% are in the noise zone.

A subsequent control measurement on qwen3.6-35b with explicit thinking muzzle (chat_template_kwargs.enable_thinking=False) under the same harness shows identical first-try counts across all three runs:

Qwen3.6 with --no-thinking, n=3: English-base subset (48 tasks): first-try 14/14/14, retry 37/37/36 Chinese subset (12 tasks): first-try 5/5/5, retry 8/11/9

First-try is fully deterministic with explicit thinking muzzle (std=0 across both subsets). Retry shows small residual variance (std 0.6–1.5 tasks) attributable to retry-history accumulation effects. The 25–35% flip rate observed without explicit muzzle is not intrinsic to WildClaw—it is thinking-mode-bleed noise.

The mechanism: thinking-mode models without explicit muzzle still produce some <think>-like tokens through chat-template interpolation, which the server-side reasoning-format strip handles inconsistently when token budgets are tight. Different runs of the "same" prompt produce slightly different thinking artifacts, which then propagate to different bash answers. With enable_thinking=False passed at the chat-template level, the model never enters the thinking mode at all, removing the noise source.

External factors that we previously hypothesized (Docker workspace state, web API timing, server-side scheduling) appear to contribute negligible additional noise once thinking is properly muzzled—at least on the first-attempt path.

Implications for the field

  1. Multi-step benchmark noise is not architectural. Earlier reports of high run-to-run variance on agent-style benchmarks may largely reflect inadvertent thinking-mode bleed in harness configurations that don't explicitly muzzle thinking.
  2. For architecture comparisons, use explicit thinking muzzle (chat_template_kwargs.enable_thinking=False) for apples-to-apples runs. This produces deterministic first-try and near-deterministic retry behavior, lowering the n required for confident gap claims.
  3. For practitioner-realistic deployments (where production prompts will trigger thinking), n≥3 still recommended; the 25–35% flip rate represents real production variance the model would exhibit.
  4. Single-run multi-step bench claims under ~10pt gap (2σ) without explicit muzzle remain unreliable. With muzzle, single-run gaps as small as ~1–2 tasks are interpretable.
Two Regimes, Both Useful

This is a useful split: one regime for architecture comparison, another for production realism. The choice between them depends on whether you want clean signal about the model or clean signal about the deployment experience.

Implications for Practitioners

The right axis for deployment decisions is VRAM tier, not per-token compute class in the abstract:

≤9GB VRAM (consumer GPU, phones, low-end laptops)

gemma4-E4B (~4–6GB) is the strongest tested model in this tier. It beats vanilla 8B baselines by 17pt on WildClaw multi-step and holds competitive single-question numbers. Finding 3 (gemma-4-26b-moe wins) does not apply here—the 26B MoE requires ~17GB+ VRAM and is simply not on the menu.

17GB+ VRAM (workstation, server, high-end consumer)

gemma-4-26b-moe at 7B activated is the strongest tested model by a substantial margin. The "MoE wins" narrative holds at this tier, but it is a compute-budget win (7B activated vs 4B), not an architecture-family win.

Four interpretation cautions

  1. Single-question benchmarks are still the cleanest matched-compute comparison axis. Their noise floor (~1pt) is low enough that architecture-tie results are robust at modest sample sizes.
  2. Multi-step benchmarks have noise floors that are conditional on thinking-mode handling. Without explicit thinking muzzle, single-run gaps under ~10pt should be replicated before acceptance and per-category breakdowns at small n are nearly meaningless. With explicit muzzle, first-try is fully deterministic and gaps as small as ~1–2 tasks are interpretable.
  3. First-try and with-retry tell different stories. First-try is most sensitive to routing-divergence and is the right metric for agent-loop deployments. With-retry smooths over per-attempt mistakes and is the right metric for batch-processing workloads.
  4. The "MoE is more efficient" narrative is conditional. MoE may be more memory-efficient for short tasks but underperforms at matched per-token compute on multi-step first-try. Higher-compute MoE is competitive or better. The decision depends on the task class and the deployment VRAM budget.

Limitations

One model per architecture variant

The matched-compute pair is one MoE × one dense+PLE. Larger-MoE result is one model. Broader sweeps across MoE/dense/PLE families would strengthen all findings.

One multi-step benchmark

WildClaw is a single harness with specific noise sources (Docker, web APIs). A second multi-step benchmark (SWE-bench, GAIA, agent-tool-use sweeps) would test whether the Finding 2 first-try gap survives different long-prompt regimes.

The 14B modified-vs-vanilla 5pt gap shrinks with replication

An earlier draft of this paper reported a +10pt 14B modified-variant retry advantage based on n=1; replication to n=3 brought it down to +5pt with t-stat 2.08 (borderline). This is itself a methodological caveat: even with a fixed harness, n=1 numbers in this domain can mislead by 5–10 task points.

Quantization variance

gemma4-E4B is Q6_K; the MoEs and 14B models are Q4_K_M. Q4 should depress capability somewhat, which makes the gemma-4-26b-moe win all the more impressive. Q6/Q4 differences are likely small relative to architecture deltas at this scale.

PLE mechanism is empirically strong but not explained

Finding 4 reports a 5σ phenomenon (4B PLE dominating 14B dense by 17pt on multi-step). We do not yet have a mechanistic story for why PLE punches above its compute weight on multi-step specifically. Companion work investigating this is in progress.

Thinking-mode handling is not matched across the comparison pair

qwen3-30b-moe runs with thinking-on (server-side strip); gemma4-E4B runs with thinking-off (chat-template muzzle) because the deepseek strip does not recognize Gemma's <channel|> tag. We pick "each model in its working config" rather than a forced-matched config. Thinking-mode handling can shift the same model's score by 10–25pt; the 16.3pt first-try gap reported in Finding 2 may shift under different thinking configurations.

What's Next

Test a non-MoE 7B-effective model

Run a Qwen3-7B/8B variant with thinking muzzled to discriminate "MoE wins at 7B compute" from "any 7B-effective model wins on WildClaw" on Finding 3.

Test SWE-bench Lite

Or similar long-multi-step benchmark, with the same matched-compute pair to validate that the Finding 2 first-try gap survives a different multi-step regime.

Replicate Finding 4 across additional multi-step benchmarks

If the PLE-dominates-14B-vanilla phenomenon generalizes, it deserves architectural investigation. If it doesn't, the WildClaw-specific result is still interesting but narrower.

Architecture choice is not a single-axis decision. Pick by deployment VRAM tier, task class (single-question vs multi-step), and metric (first-try vs retry). The right answer depends on all three.
